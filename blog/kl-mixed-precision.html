Where would you spend your last bit?

2026 · notes on low-bit LLM inference

Compress a language model hard enough and you have to make a choice you don't normally think about: a few weights keep their precision and most don't. Around two bits per weight there's no room to be faithful everywhere, so you protect a chosen few and let the rest degrade. Which few? That question turned out to be more interesting than the compression itself, and I think the standard way of answering it aims at the wrong target.

The thing that bugged me

I ran into this while pushing an open model down to very low precision. Once the pipeline worked, the obvious knob was where to spend a fixed budget of leftover bits. And almost immediately I hit the annoyance that drove everything after: I could improve the reconstruction — lower the weight error, lower perplexity — and still watch some downstream tasks get worse. Below a certain precision, "closer to the original weights" and "better at the task" stop pointing the same way. When the number you're minimizing isn't the number you care about, minimizing it harder can cost you.

A sensitivity metric is a bet on what matters

Deciding which weights to protect means scoring each one by how much its rounding error hurts — and you can't do that without saying what "hurts" means. The quick answer is weight error: guard the weights that move most when you round them. It's fast, and it never once looks at the model. Rounding a dead weight by a lot can be harmless; nudging a load-bearing one can wreck an answer. Distance in weight space isn't damage.

The better-known answer is loss curvature — the second-order, Hessian-flavored score most methods reach for. At least it runs the question through the model. What I don't trust is that it squeezes the whole output into one scalar loss and measures the curvature of that. At inference I never ship a loss value. I ship a next-token distribution, and I want that distribution — the gap between the right answer and the runner-up, the ordering of plausible continuations — to survive the compression. Curvature of a scalar has already thrown that away.

Preserve the output, not the loss

So write the goal down the way you actually mean it. Pick the inputs you care about and ask that the compressed model's next-token distribution stay close to the original's on them:

distortion = average over inputs of D( p_original(x) || p_compressed(x) )

Let D be KL and see what a small rounding error does to it. To second order, KL between two softmax distributions is a quadratic form in how the logits shift, weighted by the softmax's own Fisher matrix F = diag(p) − p pᵀ. Trace one weight's error forward to the logits and each weight picks up a score:

sensitivity(weight) ≈ average over inputs of (rounding error)² × (logit shift)ᵀ F (logit shift)

A weight is sensitive if changing it actually moves the model's output, measured the way the softmax measures distance. There's no Hessian to invert, and the "inputs you care about" clause isn't decoration: it's an average over a set you choose, so pointing it at the task you'll be judged on protects the weights that carry that behavior.

A version small enough to check

The claim is easy to state and easy to fool yourself about, so here it is on a model small enough to compute everything exactly: multinomial logistic regression on scikit-learn's 8×8 handwritten digits. Train it, then quantize the weight matrix to a coarse 7-level grid — but spend a small budget keeping the top 10% of weights at full precision. The only thing that varies is how you choose that 10%: by magnitude, by loss-gradient, or by the output-KL score above. Averaged over eight seeds:

Same quantizer, same 10% budget; only the ranking changes. Protecting by output-KL sensitivity recovers the most accuracy and moves the output distribution the least. Reproducible script (numpy + scikit-learn).

It's a toy on purpose — one linear model, one dataset, and the margins are a point or two. But the ordering is the whole point, and it's the ordering the argument predicts: guarding the biggest weights is the weakest use of the budget, the loss-gradient does better, and scoring weights by how much they move the output distribution does best. Nothing here depends on the model being small; it depends on measuring the right thing.

The damage is more structured than it looks

One more check, because the whole approach rests on an assumption: that the perturbation from compression is structured rather than diffuse noise smeared across the representation. If it's noise, no clever metric saves you. The cheapest test I could think of: take the hidden states from the original model and the compressed one and train a plain linear classifier to tell them apart. On an open model, one linear direction separates "original" from "compressed" activations at an AUC of about 0.99. The harm from compression isn't spread everywhere — it lives in a few directions, which is exactly the regime where a metric that knows where the model is fragile has something real to grab.

What I'd flag

The sensitivity score is a second-order, diagonal approximation: it ignores interactions between weights and gets shakier the further you drift from the operating point. The digits result is a sanity check on a linear model, not a large-scale claim. The probe says structure exists to exploit, not that any particular allocation wins. And "choose your inputs" cuts both ways — a lazy calibration set will confidently protect the wrong things.

Takeaway

The fix here was never a new algorithm. It was refusing to keep optimizing a proxy after I'd watched the proxy lie to me. "Which weights moved the most" is easy to compute and easy to believe. "Which weights change what the model does" is the one worth the effort, and once you write it as a divergence between output distributions it's computable too — and, at least on a model small enough to check, it spends the budget better.
